# Layering provenance and forensics to catch any AI image

No single signal identifies AI images across generators, so a robust detector must layer three things: provenance checks (C2PA manifests, IPTC tags, vendor watermarks), a diverse and calibrated passive-forensics ensemble, and a tri-state output that is allowed to say "inconclusive". Closed, hosted generators from OpenAI, Google, Adobe, Microsoft and Black Forest Labs (BFL) mostly attach C2PA, and OpenAI and Google also embed SynthID. But SynthID can be checked only through vendor tools, and open-weight models (FLUX dev, Stable Diffusion, probably Qwen-Image) carry essentially nothing. Metadata is stripped by most re-uploads and screenshots, and published removal attacks target invisible watermarks. A positive provenance hit is therefore strong evidence, while a missing one means "unknown", never "real". Passive detectors fill the gap but are brittle. Authors report 92-99% accuracy on FLUX, SD3.5 and Midjourney, while an independent zero-shot test of released checkpoints averaged only **18-31%** on the newest commercial generators. Calibration, compression-aware fusion and abstention matter as much as the choice of model. Evidence quality varies sharply across this report: many 2026 vendor facts come from secondary sources, and the notes explicitly mark what is unverified. Those flags are kept inline below. Status is as of November 2026.

## How images are made determines which traces survive

Most modern generators decode pixels through a learned autoencoder (VAE). Latent diffusion models denoise in an autoencoder's latent space, and AEROBLADE (CVPR 2024) exploits the fact that generated images are reconstructed by that autoencoder more accurately than real ones, detecting Stable Diffusion and Midjourney images without training ([AEROBLADE](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2024/html/Ricker_AEROBLADE_Training-Free_Detection_of_Latent_Diffusion_Images_Using_Autoencoder_Reconstruction_CVPR_2024_paper.html)). SD3 and FLUX.1 [dev] moved to rectified-flow transformers with 16-channel VAEs ([SD3 notes](https://awesome.papernotes.org/en/era5_genai_explosion/2024_stable_diffusion3/); secondary sources). Qwen-Image is listed as a 20B flow-matching MMDiT with a 16-channel VAE, but only from secondary sources and **unverified**. The notes infer that an AEROBLADE-style check built on SD's 4-channel autoencoder would likely miss 16-channel models unless the matching autoencoder is used; no source tested this. Autoregressive models add a different trace: they emit discrete VQ tokens, and exposure bias can yield token sequences the tokenizer never saw, which D3QE (ICCV 2025) exploits through codebook-usage statistics and quantization error ([D3QE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.05891)). GPT-4o image generation is described as autoregressive in secondary reporting, but the academic GPT-ImgEval paper says whether its decoder head is autoregressive or diffusion is unsettled ([InfoQ](https://www.infoq.com/news/2025/04/gpt-4o-images); [GPT-ImgEval](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.02782v2)). No primary architecture report was found for Gemini image, Midjourney, Janus-Pro or Emu3, and none for flow-matching-specific artifacts.

The classic fingerprint, GAN upsampling spectral peaks ([Frequency Bias, NeurIPS 2021](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2111.02447)), is the oldest and most architecture-specific. Diffusion models also show frequency bias ([Intriguing properties](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.06408)), and Tan et al. (CVPR 2024) argue the up-sampling operator leaves broader neighboring-pixel relationships than frequency peaks alone ([Tan et al.](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2024/papers/Tan_Rethinking_the_Up-Sampling_Operations_in_CNN-based_Generative_Network_for_Generalizable_CVPR_2024_paper.pdf)). Detectors trained on older generators fail on new ones because they learn shortcuts: family-specific spectra, PNG-versus-JPEG format differences, and content bias ([B-Free](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.17671v2)). New decoders erase those shortcuts while fidelity rises. Community Forensics shows that training on more generators helps even when architectures are similar ([Community Forensics](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.04125)). The notes' inference, not a sourced fact, is that foundation-model features (CLIP, DINOv2) plus content-aligned training data are the best-evidenced route to cross-architecture generalization and pure spectral cues the weakest.

A few details deserve emphasis. OpenAI is a "C2PA Conforming Generator Product" and its verification preview checks for a trusted manifest or an OpenAI SynthID mark; OpenAI states that no signal does not prove an image is human-made ([TNW](https://thenextweb.com/news/openai-c2pa-synthid-ai-image-detection-watermark)). A third-party removal-tool page also claims gpt-image-2 carries a content-specific pixel watermark, which is a commercial claim and **unverified** ([PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/remove-ai-watermarks/0.4.1/)). BFL's manifest contains the model name and version, a creation timestamp and a SHA-256 hash, but no prompt or account data, and BFL says it neither attaches nor validates credentials for self-hosted or third-party inference. This produces the industry rule of thumb the notes state: closed API or app means C2PA and/or a pixel watermark, while open weights mean nothing, with SD's DwtDct the one legacy exception. For China, the Measures for Labeling AI-Generated Synthetic Content and standard GB 45438-2025 took effect on 2025-09-01. They require implicit metadata labels (provider, content ID) plus visible labels, encourage but do not require watermarks, and oblige platforms to check them ([Bird & Bird](https://www.twobirds.com/en/insights/2025/new-ai-content-labelling-rules-in-china-what-are-they-and-how-do-they-compare-to-the-eu-ai-act); [Loeb](https://www.loeb.com/en/insights/publications/2025/03/chinas-ai-labeling-measures-and-mandatory-national-standards-take-effect-september-1)). In April 2026 the CAC said ByteDance's Jianying, Maoxiang and Jimeng AI did not adequately implement the requirements ([Invezz](https://invezz.com/news/2026/04/28/china-flags-bytedance-platforms-over-ai-content-labelling-violations/)). The exact XMP/EXIF field names for the Chinese label were not found. In the EU, the final Code of Practice (2026-06-10) calls for at least two layers of machine-readable marking, such as metadata plus watermark, with transparency duties effective 2026-08-02 ([Jones Day](https://www.jonesday.com/en/insights/2026/06/european-commission-publishes-final-code-of-practice-on-marking-and-labelling-aigenerated-content)). The notes expect more generators to add both layers, so a detector should read both. Neither the notes nor this report inspected real output files with exiftool or c2patool, so every per-vendor row is documentation or secondhand reporting rather than observed behavior.

## SynthID can be queried but not run locally, and every layer can be stripped

SynthID is a learned, post-hoc invisible watermark. The SynthID-Image paper (Google DeepMind, arXiv 2510.09263) says over 10 billion images and video frames were watermarked at the time and that the verifier was limited to trusted testers ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.09263)). Checking is possible only through vendor-hosted tools. The Gemini app has offered image verification since 2025-11-20, covering only Google-made or Google-edited content, with no published accuracy figures ([Google blog](https://blog.google/technology/ai/ai-image-verification-gemini-app/)). A SynthID Detector portal (synthid.com) reportedly opened publicly on 2026-10-07, listing OpenAI, NVIDIA and Kakao as partners and stating it is "not a general AI content detector". That comes from a single secondary source and is **unverified** ([Pillitteri](https://pasqualepillitteri.it/en/news/21499/google-synthid-detector-openai-nvidia-kakao)). No open-source image decoder or public API was found; google-deepmind/synthid-text covers text only ([GitHub](https://github.com/google-deepmind/synthid-text)). The practical consequence is that a detector can link users to these tools but cannot call SynthID programmatically, and a negative result says nothing about generators outside Google and OpenAI. Other invisible watermarks differ: DwtDct is readable by anyone with the open library, Amazon Titan offers a Bedrock `DetectGeneratedContent` API that returns a confidence score ([AWS](https://aws.amazon.com/about-aws/whats-new/2024/04/watermark-detection-amazon-titan-image-generator-bedrock/)), and Meta Seal is open (MIT) but detects only marks you embedded yourself ([Meta Seal](https://facebookresearch.github.io/meta-seal)). Tree-Ring, Gaussian Shading and Stable Signature need the generating model or inversion to detect, per the notes' background knowledge.

Robustness is where the evidence turns skeptical. UnMarker (IEEE S&P 2025), a black-box, data-free attack on spectral amplitudes, cut the best detection rate on semantic watermarks to 43% across seven schemes; these are the authors' own claims with no independent replication found ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.08363v2)). Diffusion-based editing drove exact-match detection of several learned watermarks (HiDDeN, StegaStamp, TrustMark, VINE) to near zero, though SynthID was not tested and the fetched table did not fully match the paper's text, so only the qualitative conclusion is usable ([arXiv 2510.05978](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.05978v1)). Black-box forgery of semantic watermarks also works, reaching over 84% detection for Tree-Ring on most models ([Müller et al., CVPR 2025](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2025/html/Muller_Black-Box_Forgery_Attacks_on_Semantic_Watermarks_for_Diffusion_Models_CVPR_2025_paper.html)). For SynthID specifically, evidence is weak and contested: MarkNull (arXiv 2608.10166) reportedly shows it resists a basic attack but fails against latent-space manipulation (seen only via a search summary, table partly seen) ([MarkNull](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.10166)); a community "Reverse-SynthID" spectral attack claims about 91% watermark-energy removal, which Google disputes and which is not peer reviewed ([Pillitteri](https://pasqualepillitteri.it/en/news/909/reverse-synthid-broken-google-ai-watermark-spectral-analysis)); and an anecdotal test found Nano Banana Pro still recognized an image after cleanup attempts ([Neowin](https://www.neowin.net/news/the-gemini-app-now-tells-you-if-an-image-is-ai-generated-or-not/)). No peer-reviewed SynthID removal measurement exists in the notes. Metadata is even more fragile. BFL itself says manifests can be removed, edits break the hard binding, and social platforms may strip them ([BFL help](https://help.bfl.ai/articles/3705384267-c2pa-in-flux-generated-images)). One report that seven of eight platforms stripped a C2PA video's credentials is secondary and unverified ([blog report](https://www.top10films.co.uk/89040-content-authenticity-in-2025-where-c2pa-content-credentials-really-stand/)), and percentages such as "80% of images lose metadata" could not be traced and should not be used. IPTC `DigitalSourceType` is read by Meta and Google Search ([IPTC](https://iptc.org/news/2024/02)) but is trivially stripped or forged. The asymmetry settles the verdict logic: a valid signed manifest or a vendor-own watermark hit supports "declared AI", and absence supports only "unknown". Soft-binding (watermark or fingerprint lookup to recover a manifest) exists in spec 2.x, but the notes' only source is weak ([MEXC](https://www.mexc.com/news/813273)).

## Passive detection: authors claim 92-99%, independent tests find 18-31%

The 2024-2026 literature splits into foundation-feature probes (UnivFD, FusionDetect, DRCT), low-level and spectral detectors (NPR, LaDeDa, SPAI), data-centric training (B-Free, Community Forensics, GAMMA), training-free methods (ZED, RIGID) and multimodal-LLM explainers (AIGI-Holmes), and the notes conclude data design matters more than architecture. B-Free (CVPR 2025) fine-tunes DINOv2-with-registers on 504x504 crops without resizing, using 309,102 self-conditioned SD 2.1 reconstructions that share content with 51,517 real COCO images; it reports balanced accuracy of 92.3 on FLUX, 98.9 on SD3.5, 99.6 on Midjourney and 98.2 on DALL-E 3 ([arXiv 2412.17671](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.17671v2)). SPAI (CVPR 2025) scores images as out-of-distribution relative to real-image spectra and handles any resolution, but its own table shows weaker AUC on newer systems: **83.0 on FLUX and 75.9 on SD3**, versus 99.6 on SD1.4, with the NPR baseline at 45.4 ([arXiv 2411.19417](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.19417v2)). Community Forensics trained on 2.7M images from 4,803 generators ([arXiv 2411.04125](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.04125v1.pdf)). FusionDetect, which concatenates frozen CLIP and DINOv2 features, reports 97.38% on its own 12-generator benchmark, weakest on Midjourney v7 at 87.5%, with the caveat of partly retrained baselines ([arXiv 2510.05740](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.05740v1)). All of these are author-reported figures.

The independent picture is harsher. A zero-shot benchmark of 16 detectors (23 checkpoints) on 12 datasets, including GPT-4o and Nano Banana content, found mean accuracy across detectors of **21% on Flux Dev, 18% on Firefly v4, 24% on Midjourney v7, 19% on Imagen 4 and 31% on DALL-E 3**, against 87% on ProGAN. Community-Forensics scored best at 0.78 and rankings varied wildly across datasets (Spearman 0.01-0.87) ([arXiv 2602.07814](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.07814)). That paper's abstract says 75.0 for Community-Forensics while its Table 3 says 78.0, and it did not test compression robustness. The notes therefore treat any single number as an upper bound, since authors use matched post-processing, curated test sets and tuned baselines. The notes also record a conflict: B-Free and MiraGe report high accuracy on FLUX and SD3.5, while AIGIBench and Chameleon report collapse on harder or in-the-wild sources. The notes' reading is that the difference comes from test-set curation and degradation rather than a true contradiction. Chameleon, a set of fakes that passed a human Turing test, made nine off-the-shelf detectors call nearly all of them real ([AIDE, ICLR 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.19435v3)). No source gave clean per-detector numbers on GPT-image or Gemini-image output, and a 2026 benchmark for GPT Image 2 and Nano Banana 2 (arXiv 2608.01258) was too large to fetch ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.01258)).

Degradation is the second failure mode. AIGIBench found average drops above 40% on real-world data, and under JPEG the fake accuracy of nearly all detectors fell to about 0% while real accuracy stayed near 100%, a severe bias toward "real" ([arXiv 2505.12335](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.12335)). The TrueFake figure of 32-57% average accuracy loss after social propagation is a secondary summary ([listing](https://newx.sg/paper/detail/5324f73e-f823-11f0-b84c-00163e10baa7)). What helps is documented: DINOv2-L/14 gave the best average AUC (94.90) on an in-the-wild social benchmark, in-the-wild training data lifted DMID from 79.96 to 91.55 AUC, and texture-aware cropping beat center crops ([arXiv 2507.10236](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.10236v1)). B-Free stayed near 92% over 100 days on 1,400 viral images where most competitors fell below 70% after one day, and SPAI openly fails on screenshots, memes and photos of screens. Commercial detectors show the same gap between vendor claims and independent tests. Hive led one peer-reviewed comparison at about 98% accuracy ([arXiv 2402.03214](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.03214)), but on a 200-image test it fell from 1.0 to 0.88 under Gaussian noise ([arXiv 2411.13553](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.13553)). Reported figures for AI or Not (about 84-88% versus a roughly 98.9% claim) come from low-reliability roundups ([AIMultiple](https://aimultiple.com/ai-image-detector)), and the NewsGuard audit numbers are secondhand ([secondary roundup](https://checkthat.ai/answers/what-are-the-best-ai-image-detector-tools)). No peer-reviewed independent evaluation of AI or Not or Reality Defender was found.

Two further risks shape deployment. Detectors are attackable: RAID crafted 72k adversarial examples against a seven-detector ensemble that transfer to unseen detectors, so fusing detectors does not stop an ensemble-aware attacker ([arXiv 2506.03988](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.03988v3)). And false positives on real photos can be large: LAION-Mobile (about 1M smartphone photos, abstract read only) found thresholds fit on GAN-era data gave under 11% FPR, but refit on modern content several detectors flagged 17-91% of real photos, and the dataset covers only 2018-2020 phones ([arXiv 2609.11134](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.11134)). The notes also report authentic news photos flagged as AI 13.3% of the time on average in a May 2026 NewsGuard audit, but that is secondhand and unverified ([PhotoWorkout](https://www.photoworkout.com/?p=222434)).

## What a team can reuse today, and what the licenses allow

For a permissive stack, the cleanest options are SPAI (Apache-2.0, weights released, GPU under 8 GB, last pushed 2025-08-01) ([GitHub](https://github.com/mever-team/spai)) and GRIP-UNINA's CLIP-based detector (Apache-2.0, last pushed 2024-11-24; the weights' own license was not stated) ([GitHub](https://github.com/grip-unina/ClipBased-SyntheticImageDetection)). Both were trained on latent-diffusion-era data and need revalidation on FLUX and later. UniversalFakeDetect (MIT) ([GitHub](https://github.com/WisconsinAIVision/UniversalFakeDetect)) and AIDE (MIT code; Chameleon data is request-only and academic) ([GitHub](https://github.com/shilinyan99/AIDE)) are usable, while B-Free carries a custom non-profit license, so the strongest academic detector is out for commercial use ([GitHub](https://github.com/grip-unina/B-Free)). Several repos (Effort, NPR, DRCT) declare no license, which the notes treat as all rights reserved. DIRE is archived. Community Forensics classifiers are on Hugging Face (`OwensLab/commfor-model-384`, `-224`) ([HF](https://huggingface.co/datasets/OwensLab/CommunityForensics)). Popular Hugging Face classifiers such as `umm-maybe/AI-image-detector` (68.5K downloads) are small-data fine-tunes of the pre-FLUX era with unknown generalization, and one (`Smogy`) is CC-BY-NC ([HF listing](https://huggingface.co/api/models?search=ai%20image%20detector&sort=downloads&direction=-1&limit=12)). No source validated any open detector on GPT-image, Nano Banana, Qwen-Image or Midjourney v7.

Datasets are mostly non-commercial. OpenFake (about 4M images; FLUX.1/FLUX.2, Imagen 4, HiDream, SD variants; cc-by-nc-4.0; adversarial crowdsourcing platform) has the newest coverage ([HF](https://huggingface.co/datasets/ComplexDataLab/OpenFake)). AIGIBench (FLUX1-dev, Midjourney-V6, SD3, Imagen-3, plus wild subsets) is cc-by-nc-sa ([GitHub](https://github.com/HorizonTEL/AIGIBench)). WildRF offers a social-media protocol, with LaDeDa reporting 93.7% mAP, a model that predates 2025 generators ([arXiv 2406.09398](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.09398v1)). None confirmed GPT-image, Gemini or Qwen-Image content, so a team must generate its own. The provenance tooling is the most dependable open code: c2pa-rs, c2patool (Apache-2.0/MIT) and c2pa-python (Apache-2.0) are actively maintained, with pushes in late October 2026 ([c2pa-python](https://github.com/contentauth/c2pa-python); [c2patool](https://github.com/contentauth/c2patool)). The `invisible-watermark` library (MIT) decodes DwtDct but has not been updated since 2023 ([GitHub](https://github.com/ShieldMnt/invisible-watermark)). Unofficial SynthID reverse-engineering projects exist (reverse-SynthID, over 5,000 stars) and are unverified third-party claims ([GitHub](https://github.com/aloshdenny/reverse-SynthID)). TrustMark and Stable Signature licenses were not read.

## A seven-layer design that treats "no signal" as unknown

The design below is mostly the notes' inference, supported by the evidence above, and no published end-to-end evaluation of this exact stack exists. Cost and latency were not researched.

**Layer 0 and 1, triage and provenance.** First estimate JPEG history, resolution, palette or transparency, and screenshot or upscale hints, and record them as features and out-of-domain flags. The existing repo already treats palette, transparent and tiny images as out of domain. Then validate any C2PA manifest with c2pa-python or c2patool: signature chain, trusted issuer, hard-binding hash, and the `digitalSourceType` assertion. Read IPTC/XMP `trainedAlgorithmicMedia`, DwtDct, and the likely China GB 45438 metadata label (field names still unknown). A valid AI assertion or vendor-own watermark yields "declared AI". A valid camera claim only lowers the prior, since content can be edited or spoofed. SynthID cannot be run locally, so the tool links to synthid.com, openai.com/verify or the Gemini app, and non-declared verdicts for other generators should point to vendor verifiers.

**Layer 2 and 3, passive ensemble and calibrated fusion.** Combine diverse detectors: a DINOv2/CLIP-feature model, a low-level artifact model, and a spectral model like SPAI, using texture-aware multi-crop inference and in-the-wild, recompressed training data. Fuse in logit space with logistic regression or a small gradient-boosted model that also takes compression and resolution features. One calibration paper finds fake logits shift globally under distribution shift, so a single learned bias fit on a few target samples can lift a detector (+16.16% for RINE on GenImage) ([arXiv 2602.01973](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.01973v1)). Set thresholds from real-image quantiles on matched processing at a fixed low FPR, because a threshold at 0.5% FPR yielding about 98% TPR in distribution dropped to 84.5% on an unseen synthesis engine ([arXiv 2311.08577](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2311.08577)). Learned scores near 0.5 should not vote, as in the repo's fusion v0.3, and the system should abstain on out-of-distribution input, strong detector disagreement, extreme recompression, or detected upscaling and inpainting. A "processing class" side-head (AI-upscaled, heavily edited, mobile computational photo, inpainted) should route to abstain or "edited", since partial edits are a documented blind spot ([Stay-Positive](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.07778)) and upscaling claims rest on blogs only. Output is tri-state, declared AI, likely real, or inconclusive, and never "real" with certainty. No published convention exists for abstention in AI-image detection, and conformal abstention has not been applied there.

**Layer 4 to 6, retrieval, updating and adversarial hygiene.** Near-duplicate lookup can recover a less-degraded copy, as QuAD does for reposts ([arXiv 2604.15027](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.15027v1)). Update monthly with fresh outputs from new closed models, keep a frozen and a rolling held-out set, use adapter updates with replay, and refit the fusion and calibration layers after each update; continual-learning numbers such as DECODE's 0.39% forgetting are author-reported ([arXiv 2601.05580](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.05580)). Keep some weights private, randomize crops, rate-limit queries, and red-team with RAID-style transfer attacks and low-strength regeneration. Regeneration is the cheapest attack on watermarks but may leave a diffusion-edit trace that passive detectors still catch, which is the core argument for layering passive over provenance.

**Evaluation.** AUC alone is misleading. Report TPR at 0.1% and 1% FPR per generator and per degradation, FPR on real photos by source (including modern phones with HDR and night mode, which public sets underrepresent), and coverage-versus-risk for abstention. Test through real platform re-upload pipelines, not only synthetic JPEG sweeps; no published per-platform quantification of detector TPR or metadata survival was found, and screenshots were not covered by any source.

## Conclusion

The research reframes the problem from "build a better classifier" to "decide what each signal can legitimately prove". Provenance is asymmetric, since presence is strong and absence is empty, while passive detection is symmetric but brittle, and independent tests show released detectors near or below chance on the newest commercial generators. The regulatory trend (EU two-layer marking, China's mandatory metadata) will raise the share of images carrying readable labels, but it does nothing for open weights, which per BFL's own policy carry no credentials by design. The leverage points are therefore calibration, abstention and a pipeline that keeps adding fresh generator samples, rather than any single architecture.

The largest open uncertainties are factual rather than methodological. Real outputs from Grok, Midjourney, Qwen-Image, Seedream and Ideogram were never inspected, SynthID's real-world removal rate has no peer-reviewed measurement, and there are no independent per-detector numbers on GPT-image or Gemini-image. The cheapest next step is empirical: generate samples from each target model, run c2patool and exiftool on them, pass them through real social platforms, and measure the shortlisted open detectors (SPAI, GRIP CLIP, Community Forensics) on that data before trusting any published figure.
